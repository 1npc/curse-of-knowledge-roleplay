\title{Role-Playing Models Leak What Their Characters Cannot Access}
\author{Anonymous ACL submission}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Role-playing language models voice characters in games and agent simulations, where the system records what each character can hear, see and understand. We test whether a character's speech stays within these boundaries. Each scene has a counterfactual twin that changes the hidden fact, which separates use of inaccessible content from guessing within the same replies. Across 12 open-weight models, characters repeat speech they cannot understand or cannot hear, with net use averaging 67 and 72 points, although the same models answer correctly when asked what the character can perceive. The failure extends to hidden actions and private messages, and the leaked facts change where characters propose to search. Five of six frontier hosted models rarely leak while still using permitted content, so the boundary can be kept. This compliance depends on the input format, however. A single change to the scene layout or the character description raises leakage in strong open models from under 10 to over 60 points, and combined changes raise it in Gemini 3.1 Pro from 0 to 21. A filter that first lists what the character perceives removes 65\% of leakage on average, and it fails mainly where that list already contains the hidden content.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A non-player character who does not speak Spanish overhears two mechanics say, in Spanish, that a mirror is hidden in the warehouse. When the player asks what they said, the character answers that they were talking about a mirror in the warehouse (Figure~\ref{fig:example}). Readers see the error at once, because the character heard the words without understanding them. The model that voices the character, however, receives the Spanish sentence, understands it, and is asked to speak as someone who does not.

% INSERT FIG_EXAMPLE

This situation is built into how role-playing systems work. Games and agent simulations keep a world state that records who is present, who can hear whom and which languages each character speaks, and they pass that state to a model that voices one character at a time \citep{park2023generative,zhou2024sotopia}. The model therefore sees everything, while the character should know only part of it. Whether the model respects that difference decides whether secrets stay secret, whether a language barrier has any effect, and whether simulated agents act on what they could actually know.

Prior work shows that models can state a perspective without acting on it. They answer questions about what a story character knows yet mispredict the character's behavior \citep{gu2026simpletom}, and they recognize the limits of a role's knowledge yet answer beyond them \citep{han2026charm}. These studies ask questions about stories or about knowledge stored in the model's parameters, and they evaluate each model with one prompt format. We ask a question closer to deployment. When the inaccessible content sits in the model's own input and the boundary is stated in the character's state, does the content reach the character's speech, and does the answer change with the format of the input?

We measure this with counterfactual scenes. Every scene has two versions that differ only in a hidden object and place, and the other version's values serve as a control for guessing within the same replies (Section~\ref{sec:measure}). Study~1 places 11 open models in NPC-style prompts and varies whether the character cannot understand or cannot hear the speech, and how the boundary is stated. Study~2 extends the question to hidden actions and private messages in a minimal event format, adds delayed questions and a search choice, and replicates on scenes with original characters. We then run six frontier hosted models and a large open model on the core conditions, and we swap individual features between the two input formats to find what makes models comply.

The results separate reading a boundary from keeping it. Open models use content their characters cannot perceive in most replies, and they do so for unheard speech even more than for speech in an unknown language, while answering questions about the state correctly. Most frontier models keep the boundary and still use permitted content, which shows that the task is solvable. Their compliance and that of the strongest open models depends on incidental parts of the input, such as how the scene state is laid out or how the character is described, and the feature that matters differs from model to model. Our contribution is an evaluation of this behavior and of the conditions under which it appears. Items, prompts, the scorer and all responses will be released.

\section{Measuring What a Character Uses}
\label{sec:measure}
Each scene contains one fact that names an object and a place. Version B replaces both values and keeps everything else, including the character, the state and the question (Figure~\ref{fig:design}). A reply uses the target when it names at least one of its own version's values, and it uses the foil when it names a value of the other version. Net use $D=I(\text{target})-I(\text{foil})$ is averaged over models and over scene skeletons and reported in percentage points (pp). Because the foil is scored on the same replies, a plausible guess such as ``check the basement'' adds to both terms and cancels. A rule-based scorer matches a dictionary of 12 places and 12 objects with their synonyms, removes echoed foreign text, and counts a reply that names two values of one type as neither. Scenes come in two forms. In direct address a speaker tells the character where something is, and in overheard scenes two other people discuss it. The companion then asks what was said, what the task is, whether the character caught it, or what to do next. Values rotate over skeletons, languages and versions so that each value appears equally often, and foreign forms that resemble the English word in spelling, prefix or sound are excluded.

% INSERT FIG_DESIGN

Two probes run as separate calls. A translation probe asks the model to translate the foreign sentence, which shows that the model can understand the content at all, and a state probe asks out of character whether the character could hear and understand the speech. On 150 replies labeled blind by a human annotator across both studies, human and scorer agree on target use in 146 ($\kappa=0.95$), neither finds a foil use, and net use differs by at most 2.8 pp in any condition (Appendix~\ref{app:audit}). Intervals resample scene skeletons jointly across models with 2,000 draws.

The open models are 11 checkpoints of at most 40B parameters from nine families, plus DeepSeek-V4-Pro as a large open model. The hosted models are GPT-6 Sol, Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Sonnet 5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Gemini 3.8 Flash and Grok 4.7, run through OpenRouter. Open models decode greedily with thinking disabled, and hosted models use the lowest reasoning setting each endpoint allows (Appendix~\ref{app:models}).

\section{Open Models Cross Both Boundaries}
\label{sec:s1}
In Study~1, a character from a role-playing game (Kerry, Rogue or Panam) hears a short utterance, addressed to them or overheard, and a companion who missed it asks about it. The system prompt contains a character sheet and a JSON scene state with the speech events and the character's languages. The speech is either in Chinese, Japanese or Spanish, which the character does not speak, or in English marked as inaudible. The boundary is stated in one of the five ways shown in Figure~\ref{fig:design}, and controls make the same speech accessible through fluency, a working translator or audibility. The design has 96 skeletons in three languages (288 items with 143 prompts each), and every model passed the translation probe for every language. We fixed eight tests after a pilot on separate items and locked the analysis before scoring. They cover leakage under each boundary, the effect of prose, the comparison between boundaries, the derivation from the repertoire, leakage when the state is read correctly, and the perspective filter, and all eight hold (Table~\ref{tab:s1}).

Characters use the hidden content in most replies under both boundaries (Figure~\ref{fig:s1}). When the state lists only the character's languages, net use is 66.7 pp [64.9, 68.5], and when English speech carries only an inaudibility flag it is 72.2 pp [69.0, 75.5], with every model above 45 pp in both conditions. Unheard speech is repeated as if the character had been there, as in Gemma-4-31B's ``Some drunken rambling about losing a glove in a sewer'' for speech its state marks as inaudible. Replacing the speech with noise of the same form yields 0.3\% target hits, so the use comes from the content. The effect remains in a present-day setting with original characters (50.1 pp), under three other system prompts, and under sampling at temperature 0.7, which moves four models' estimates by at most 4 pp. Most leaks are stated as fact (67\% of target uses, as in ``Bag's in the basement''), 17\% are attributed to the speaker, and 16\% are hedged. The question does not need to ask for the content. When the companion only asks whether the character caught what was said, characters reveal it in 52.7 pp of replies, and when asked what to do next in 50.1 pp, against 81.7 pp when asked what was said (Figure~\ref{fig:questions}). Direct address leaks 8.3 pp more than overheard talk, and Spanish leaks 4.5 to 5.7 pp more than Chinese or Japanese.

The comparison between boundaries runs against intuition. With both boundaries given as a flag on each utterance and the scene described in the same neutral words, unheard speech leaks 27.1 pp more than speech marked as not understood. Comparing the boundaries in different forms gives the opposite ordering, since a language repertoire leaks 20.3 pp more than inaudibility narrated in the scene, which shows that boundary types can only be compared when they are stated the same way. The form also matters within the language boundary. Models do not derive incomprehension from the repertoire, because a direct comprehension flag lowers net use by 21.6 pp. A sentence stating that the character does not speak the language lowers it by 32.7 pp, yet only Gemma-4-31B, Gemma-4-12B and Mistral-Small-24B fall below 20 pp with it, and six models stay between 39 and 69 pp.

The models read the boundary correctly. Ten of them answer the state probe correctly for 87--100\% of items, and on exactly those items net use is 68.4 pp. GLM-4.7-Flash is the exception, since it answers that the character understands in all 288 items. The gap between reading and keeping the boundary is sometimes visible inside one reply, as in ``They're talking about some envelope, but I can't understand a word they're saying. Something about the parking lot, though.'' Within Qwen3.5, leakage under the repertoire rises with model size from 13.9 pp at 0.8B to 82.9 pp at 9B, so a model that understands the language better gives its character more of it.

\section{The Failure Extends to Sight, Messages and Actions}
\label{sec:s2}
Study~2 asks whether the same failure appears when content is hidden for other reasons and whether it shapes what characters do. A minimal JSON scene contains one event with an actor, the content and an access flag, and the character either cannot hear English speech, cannot understand Chinese, Japanese or Spanish speech, cannot see where an object is put, or cannot read a private message. Accessible controls change only the flag, and Table~\ref{tab:accesscases} lists what the character can still observe in each channel. A discovery set of 96 scenes runs on all 11 open models with three state representations, and a confirmation set of 96 new scenes runs on the same models. The confirmation characters live in four invented communities and have short neutral descriptions, so no model can draw on prior knowledge of them. Inputs, models, tests and analysis were fixed before generation for a cohort of four models, and the other seven were added afterwards.

% INSERT S2_ACCESS
% INSERT FIG_S2

The failure appears in every channel (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}a). Over the 11 models in the confirmation set, net use is 74.7 pp for unheard speech, 35.6 pp for speech in an unknown language, 60.6 pp for hidden actions and 72.0 pp for private messages. Among replies whose state probe the model answered correctly, it remains 60.7 pp [59.6, 61.7], and the four-model cohort fixed in advance shows the same pattern with 72.3, 47.2, 69.3 and 67.6 pp (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:confirm}). Profiles differ by channel within a model. Mistral-Small-24B almost never uses speech it cannot understand, yet it repeats unheard speech and private messages in most replies, and Gemma-4-12B keeps hidden actions out of its speech (24 pp) while repeating private messages (79 pp). How the boundary is written matters in every channel (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}c). A direct flag leaks 18--33 pp less than a list of who can perceive what for hearing, comprehension and sight, but only 3.4 pp less for private messages. Adding a prose rule to the flag lowers leakage by 18--38 pp for hearing, sight and private messages, while for comprehension it changes leakage by only 4.0 pp, which matches the uneven effect of the prose sentence in Study~1.



Leaked facts also guide behavior, as tests on the four-model cohort show. With the event kept in the conversation, leakage stays between 51 and 71 pp after four neutral exchanges and rises by 15.6 pp for comprehension (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}d). When characters choose between two places to search, switching the inaccessible fact from B to A raises the choice of place A by 49--63 pp (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}b).

\section{Strong Models Keep the Boundary in Some Formats}
\label{sec:frontier}
Gemma-4-31B and Qwen3.8-27B use unheard speech in 51\% and 60\% of Study~1 replies, yet in Study~2's discovery set they leak 4 and 9 pp for the same boundary. Frontier models raise the same question at a larger scale. We ran six hosted models and DeepSeek-V4-Pro on the core Study~1 conditions with the content question, and on both ends of a bridge experiment that moves Study~2's hearing prompts toward Study~1.

% INSERT FIG_FRONTIER
% INSERT BRIDGE_TABLE

Five of the six hosted models rarely use the hidden content (Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}a, Appendix Table~\ref{tab:hosted}). GPT-6 Sol, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Grok 4.7 and Claude Opus 5.5 stay at or below 2.1 pp in both conditions and Gemini 3.1 Pro at or below 9.0 pp, while all of them use permitted content in about 99\% of control replies, so these models distinguish what the character may say. Claude Sonnet 5.5 reaches 26.0 pp under the repertoire and 35.8 pp with a comprehension flag. Its leaks are mostly framed as guesses (65\%), for example ``Something about an envelope, maybe? I definitely heard `envelope' in there.'' A compliant reply stays in character and declines the content, as in Claude Opus 5.5's ``Couldn't tell you. He said it in Spanish, and I don't speak it. Ask him to say it again in English, or get a translator running.'' Every hosted model answers the state probe correctly in at least 96\% of items and translates both hidden values in at least 92\%, so the leaks do not come from misreading. DeepSeek-V4-Pro behaves like the smaller open models, at 71.2 and 78.3 pp, and it also hedges most of its leaks. Figure~\ref{fig:framing} shows that this hedging separates the two strongest leaking models from the smaller ones, whose leaks are mostly stated as fact or attributed to the speaker.

% INSERT FIG_FRAMING


Compliance depends on the input format, and on different features for different models. Starting from Study~1's prompts, adding Study~2's channel field or its direct question changes leakage by at most 4 pp, and removing the character sheet and scene description lowers it by 11.0 pp for unheard speech and 21.5 pp for speech not understood, yet Gemma-4-31B still repeats unheard speech in 50\% of replies (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). The two formats differ in how the state is organized. Study~2 lists events, each with an actor, its content and an access field, whereas Study~1 places speech events with a speaker, an addressee and the text under a scene description and adds the character's languages and a full character sheet. The reverse direction locates the cause. Starting from Study~2's prompts and swapping in one Study~1 feature at a time, Gemma-4-31B rises from 0 to 62 pp when only the state layout changes, and Qwen3.8-27B rises from 9 to 68 pp when only the character sheet changes (Table~\ref{tab:bridge}). With all four features swapped, Gemma-4-31B, Qwen3.8-27B and DeepSeek-V4-Pro exceed 65 pp, and among hosted models Gemini 3.1 Pro rises from 0 to 21 pp and Claude Sonnet 5.5 from 0.5 to 13 pp (Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}b). Throughout, these models keep using audible speech in 80--100\% of replies, so the low leakage under Study~2's prompt reflects the boundary and not a general reluctance to report speech.

\section{Filtering the Character's View}
\label{sec:remedy}
If models leak because they speak from the whole scene, giving them only the character's view should help. We tested a prompted filter and a deterministic one.

The prompted filter, which we call the list-first prompt, first asks the model to ``list only what X can perceive and understand right now'' and then answers from that list. In Study~1 it reduces leakage by 64.7\% on average and changes the use of speech made accessible by a translator by $-2.0$ pp (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:s1m}). The models that it does not fix are the ones whose lists already contain the hidden values (Figure~\ref{fig:remedy}). Gemma-4-31B, Gemma-4-12B, Qwen3.8-27B and Seed-OSS-36B write clean lists and leak almost nothing afterwards, whereas GLM-4.7-Flash and Qwen3.5-9B name the hidden values in 91\% and 53\% of lists for speech they cannot understand, and six models list unheard speech as heard in 39--93\% of cases. A clean list from Gemma-4-31B reads ``Kerry perceives a street kid walking up to him and speaking aloud, but he cannot understand the words spoken.'' A list from GLM-4.7-Flash for the same character contains an entry headed Understanding that translates the Japanese, ``The roadies are discussing a singer having forgotten their bag again and that it is at the church.'' Mistral-Small-24B is the one model whose answers leak (45 pp) although its lists are mostly clean. Two in-world examples of a character who does not understand a language work for Gemma-4-31B but make Gemma-4-12B ignore speech it may understand, and its use of translated speech falls from 80\% to 22\%.

% INSERT FIG_REMEDY

When the system knows what the character can access, it can build the input from that knowledge. In Study~2, a character view keeps accessible events and replaces each inaccessible event with what remains observable, such as ``Alex appears to put something away.'' Over all 11 models this removes 60.7 pp of leakage and changes the use of accessible content by $+0.6$ pp [0.2, 1.0], and with it the leaked facts no longer move search choices (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}b). Removing the event entirely also stops leakage, but the character then cannot report that anything happened (32\% compared with 100\% under the view). The prompted counterpart can fail in the opposite direction. Across the six models in Study~2's method comparison, self-filtering lowers the use of permitted content by 28.6 pp for comprehension and 41.2 pp for private messages, against 3.7 and 3.3 pp for the character view. Qwen3.8-27B's self-filter, for example, returns only ``Casey says something in Japanese'' even when the character speaks Japanese, so its use of permitted content falls from 86\% to 0\%.

\section{Related Work}
Role-playing agents are trained and evaluated for consistency with a persona \citep{shao-etal-2023-character,wang-etal-2024-rolellm}, and benchmarks score that consistency through dialogue quality or psychological interviews \citep{tu-etal-2024-charactereval,wang-etal-2024-incharacter}. Work on character hallucination tests whether a role answers beyond its time period or fictional world \citep{ahn-etal-2024-timechara,han2026charm}, perspective-bounded memory restricts a book character to the facts it witnessed \citep{tang2026staying}, and separate role memories keep information from flowing between roles \citep{zhu2026mentor}. PatientSim conditions a simulated patient on language proficiency \citep{kyung2025patientsim}. In our setting the boundary comes from the current scene, and the content is fully present in the input.

Theory-of-mind benchmarks test reasoning about information asymmetry \citep{kim-etal-2023-fantom}, separate perception inference from belief inference \citep{jung-etal-2024-perceptions}, and show that models answer mental-state questions without applying the answers \citep{gu2026simpletom}. These benchmarks ask third-person questions with a gold answer, whereas we score first-person speech for content the character could not know. The behavior resembles the curse of knowledge in people \citep{birch2007curse}. ConfAIde and PrivacyLens find that models disclose information in context against norms they can state \citep{mireshghallah2024confaide,shao2024privacylens}, which has the same structure with fictional access in place of privacy. Small formatting changes shift task accuracy \citep{sclar2024quantifying}, and we find that they also decide whether a model keeps a character's boundary. Perspective filtering rewrites the context from a character's point of view before answering \citep{wilf-etal-2024-think}, and the list-first prompt applies this idea to generation.

\section{Discussion}
Why would a model that answers the state probe correctly still voice the content? One explanation is that it writes as a narrator who has seen the whole scene. Fiction often renders foreign speech for the reader and reports what characters failed to notice, so continuing a scene from the full state is the familiar task and keeping a character's view is the unusual one. Three observations agree with this reading. Open models mostly state leaked content as fact, leakage grows with the model's command of the language, and the boundary holds when the task is about the boundary, as in the state probe and in the first step of the list-first prompt, but not when the task is to talk.

The format results change how such behavior should be evaluated. A model that keeps the boundary under one prompt can break it when the state is laid out differently or when the character gains a backstory, and the feature that matters differs between models. A single prompt therefore cannot certify a model. Evaluations should vary the input format and report the use of permitted content next to leakage, because a model that never reports any speech would otherwise score perfectly.

The same failure matters beyond games. Agent simulations study how information spreads, how rumors form and how agents coordinate under partial knowledge \citep{park2023generative,zhou2024sotopia}. If each agent's model acts on the full state, the information asymmetry that such simulations are meant to study disappears, and their conclusions describe agents that know everything.

The hosted models that comply show that keeping the boundary can be learned. Their training is not public, so we cannot tell which data or objective produces it, but the open models that come closest show that compliance learned for one format does not carry over to another.

For system builders, the dependable remedy is to keep inaccessible content out of the generator's input. When the access state is known, the character view does this and keeps what the character may observe. When it is not known, a perspective filter is the practical option, and its intermediate list is the place to check for failure.

\section{Conclusion}
Role-playing models read a character's perceptual boundary correctly and still let the hidden content into the character's speech and choices. The failure holds across open models and across channels, and it is not specific to language. Most frontier models keep the boundary, but whether a model keeps it depends on parts of the input that a designer would not consider part of the boundary.

\section*{Limitations}
Content use is scored by rules over a fixed vocabulary of 12 places and 12 objects, and one human annotator checked 150 replies. Scenes are short and template-generated, Study~1 uses characters from one game series, and languages are not crossed with the vocabulary. Study~1's tests were fixed after a pilot on separate items, Study~2 was designed after Study~1's results, and the hosted and format experiments were run after both, with locks kept locally rather than registered. Hosted models were run on the content question only, with reasoning at the lowest setting their endpoints allow, and Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemini 3.1 Pro were not run on the comprehension-flag and prose conditions. The character view assumes correct access metadata.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
All scenes, facts and conversations are fictional. Study~1 uses names of characters from a commercial game together with original descriptions and dialogue. One human annotator labeled 150 model replies about fictional facts. The annotator is a colleague of the authors and not an author. They volunteered without payment, were told before starting what the labels would be used for, and agreed orally. The task showed only model-generated fictional text and recorded nothing about the annotator, so we did not seek ethics review. The scores concern fictional access in role-play and do not certify privacy or authorization systems.
\paragraph{AI assistance.} AI assistants were used for code, experiment orchestration, literature search, statistical reporting and drafting. Provisional AI annotations appear only as screening diagnostics. The authors are responsible for the content.
