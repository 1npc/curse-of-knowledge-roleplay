\title{Role-Playing Models Leak What Their Characters Cannot Access}
\author{Anonymous ACL submission}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Role-playing language models speak as characters in games and agent simulations, where the system records what each character can hear, see and understand. We test whether characters respect these boundaries in 480 scenes, each with a counterfactual twin that changes only the hidden fact, so mentions of the twin's fact measure guessing. Across 11 open-weight models, characters use content from speech they cannot understand or hear at 67 and 72 percentage points (pp) above this baseline, although most models correctly state what the character perceives. The leak extends to hidden actions and private messages and changes where characters search. Five of six proprietary models rarely leak, but compliance in strong models depends on input format. Changing only the scene layout or character description raises the rate in the strongest open-weight models from under 10 to over 60 pp, and combined changes raise two proprietary models from near zero to 13 and 21 pp. Models write the character's line as the scene's author would, and thinking rarely helps. Giving them only what the character observes therefore stops the leak without hiding what it may report. We will release the scenes, prompts, scorer and replies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A non-player character who does not understand Spanish overhears two mechanics say, in Spanish, that a mirror is hidden in the warehouse. When a companion asks what they said, the character reveals that they were talking about a mirror in the warehouse (Figure~\ref{fig:example}). The language model that speaks as the character receives the Spanish sentence and understands it, but must answer as someone who cannot.

% INSERT FIG_EXAMPLE

This mismatch comes from how role-playing systems are built. Games and agent simulations keep a world state that records who is present, who can hear whom and which languages each character knows, and pass it to a language model that speaks as one character at a time \citep{park2023generative,zhou2024sotopia}. The model therefore receives information that the character cannot access. Whether it keeps this distinction decides whether secrets stay hidden, whether language barriers matter, and whether simulated agents act only on what they could know. The distinction is part of the design in deduction and stealth games, whose puzzles rest on what each character could have learned, and in social simulations that study how information spreads between agents.

Prior work shows that models recognize a character's perspective and still fail to act on it. They answer what a story character knows yet mispredict the character's behavior \citep{gu2026simpletom}, and they recognize the limits of a role's knowledge yet answer beyond them \citep{han2026charm}. These studies pose closed questions about a short story or about knowledge stored in the model's parameters. We study a setting closer to deployment, where the inaccessible content sits in the model's own input, the system states the character's boundary, and the model speaks freely as the character. Does that content reach the character's speech and choices? Does the answer change with the format of the input?

We measure this with counterfactual scenes. Each scene has two versions that differ only in a hidden object and place, and mentions of the other version's values give a baseline for guessing within the same replies (Section~\ref{sec:measure}). Study~1 tests whether characters use speech they cannot understand or cannot hear, across 11 open-weight models and five ways of stating the boundary. Study~2 extends the test to hidden actions and private messages and asks whether leaked facts persist over turns and change where characters search. To find what makes models comply, we run six proprietary models and a large open-weight model on the core conditions and swap single features between the two studies' input formats. Study~3 asks why models leak by changing who the model writes as and by enabling thinking. Finally, we compare prompted and deterministic ways of filtering what the character sees.

The results separate recognizing a boundary from keeping it. Open-weight models use content their characters cannot perceive in most replies, more for unheard speech than for speech in an unknown language, although most of them correctly identify what the character perceives. Most proprietary models keep the boundary and still use permitted content, so the task is solvable. Yet compliance is fragile. Gemma-4-31B leaks unheard speech at 51 pp in Study~1 and at most 4 pp in Study~2, and moving Study~1's state layout alone into Study~2's prompt brings the leak back. For the strongest open-weight models and two proprietary models, compliance depends on incidental parts of the input, such as the scene layout or the character description, and the feature that matters differs by model. Writing a character's line as the scene's author leaks nearly as much as role-play, and thinking rarely changes this, whereas building the input from the character's view removes the leak. Our contribution is an evaluation of when role-playing models cross a character's access boundary, which input features their compliance depends on, and which filters prevent the leak. We will release the items, prompts, scorer and all responses.

\section{Measurement}
\label{sec:measure}
Each scene contains one fact that names an object and a place. Version B replaces both values and keeps everything else, including the character, the state and the question (Figure~\ref{fig:example}). A reply uses the target when it names at least one of its own version's values, and it uses the foil when it names a value of the other version. Net use $D=I(\text{target})-I(\text{foil})$ is averaged over models and over scene skeletons and reported in percentage points (pp). A net use of 100 pp means that every reply names its own scene's fact and none names the twin's. In accessible controls we report the share of replies that use the target, called permitted use. Because the foil is scored on the same replies, a plausible guess such as ``check the basement'' adds to both terms and cancels. A rule-based scorer matches a dictionary of 12 places and 12 objects with their synonyms, removes echoed foreign text, and counts a reply that names two values of one type as neither. Scenes come in two forms. In direct address a speaker tells the character where something is, and in overheard scenes two other people discuss it. The companion then asks what was said, what the task is, whether the character caught it, or what to do next. Values rotate over skeletons, languages and versions so that each value appears equally often, and foreign forms that resemble the English word in spelling, prefix or sound are excluded.

% INSERT FIG_DESIGN

Two probes run as separate calls. A translation probe asks the model to translate the foreign sentence, which shows that the model can understand the content at all. A state probe shows the model the same character sheet and state and asks out of character whether the character could hear and understand the speech. On 150 replies labeled blind by a human annotator across both studies, human and scorer agree on target use in 146 ($\kappa=0.95$), neither finds a foil use, and net use differs by at most 2.8 pp in any condition (Appendix~\ref{app:audit}). Intervals resample scene skeletons jointly across models with 2,000 draws.

The open-weight models are 11 checkpoints of at most 40B parameters from nine families (Falcon-H1-34B, Gemma-4-12B, Gemma-4-31B, GLM-4.7-Flash, gpt-oss-20b, Mistral-Small-24B, Nemotron-3-Nano, Phi-4, Qwen3.5-9B, Qwen3.8-27B and Seed-OSS-36B), plus DeepSeek-V4-Pro as a large open-weight model. The proprietary models are GPT-6 Sol, Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Sonnet 5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Gemini 3.8 Flash and Grok 4.7. Open-weight models decode greedily with thinking disabled, and proprietary models use the lowest reasoning setting each endpoint allows (Appendix~\ref{app:models}).

\section{Study 1: Language and Hearing}
\label{sec:s1}
In Study~1, a character from a role-playing game (Kerry, Rogue or Panam) hears a short utterance, addressed to them or overheard, and a companion who missed it asks about it. The system prompt contains a character sheet and a JSON scene state with the speech events and the character's languages. The speech is either in Chinese, Japanese or Spanish, which the character does not speak, or in English marked as inaudible. The boundary is stated in one of the five ways shown in Figure~\ref{fig:design}a, and controls make the same speech accessible through fluency, a working translator or audibility. The design has 96 skeletons in three languages (288 items with 143 prompts each), and every model passed the translation probe for every language. Eight tests cover net use under each boundary, the effect of prose, the comparison between boundaries, the derivation from the repertoire, net use when the state is read correctly, and the perspective filter, and all eight hold (Table~\ref{tab:s1}).

% INSERT FIG_S1
% INSERT S1_TABLE

Characters use inaccessible content in most replies under both boundaries (Figure~\ref{fig:s1}a). When the state lists only the character's languages, net use is 66.7 pp [64.9, 68.5], and when English speech carries only an inaudibility flag it is 72.2 pp [69.0, 75.5], with every model above 45 pp in both conditions. Unheard speech is repeated as if the character had been there, as in Gemma-4-31B's ``Some drunken rambling about losing a glove in a sewer'' for speech its state marks as inaudible. Replacing the speech with noise of the same form yields 0.3\% target hits, so the use comes from the content. The effect remains in a present-day setting with original characters, where net use under the repertoire is 50.1 pp, under three other system prompts, and under sampling at temperature 0.7, which moves four models' estimates by at most 4 pp. Under the repertoire, most leaks are stated as fact (55\%, as in ``Bag's in the basement''), 29\% are attributed to the speaker, and 17\% are hedged. The question does not need to ask for the content. When the companion only asks whether the character caught what was said, characters reveal it in 52.7 pp of replies, and when asked what to do next in 50.1 pp, against 81.7 pp when asked what was said (Figure~\ref{fig:s1}c).

The comparison between boundaries runs against intuition. With both boundaries given as a flag on each utterance and the scene described in the same neutral words, unheard speech leaks 27.1 pp more than speech marked as not understood. Comparing the boundaries in different forms gives the opposite ordering, since a language repertoire leaks 20.3 pp more than inaudibility narrated in the scene. Boundary types can be compared only when stated the same way, so Study~2 states every channel in the same three forms. The form also matters within the language boundary. Models do not derive incomprehension from the repertoire, because a direct comprehension flag lowers net use by 21.6 pp. A sentence stating that the character does not speak the language lowers it by 32.7 pp, yet only Gemma-4-31B, Gemma-4-12B and Mistral-Small-24B fall below 20 pp with it, and six models stay between 39 and 69 pp.

Most models read the boundary correctly (Figure~\ref{fig:s1}b). For the language boundary, ten of the 11 answer the state probe correctly for 87--100\% of items, and on exactly those items net use is 68.4 pp, against 66.7 pp over all items. GLM-4.7-Flash is the exception, since it answers that the character understands in all 288 items, so its 50 pp under the repertoire reflects a misreading of the state. For hearing, nine models answer correctly for at least 87\% of items, while Phi-4 and Nemotron-3-Nano do so for only 34\% and 54\%, so part of their net use for unheard speech comes from misreading the flag. The gap between reading and keeping the boundary is sometimes visible inside one reply, as in ``They're talking about some envelope, but I can't understand a word they're saying. Something about the parking lot, though.'' Scale does not remove the failure (Figure~\ref{fig:factors}a, b). Within Qwen3.5, net use under the repertoire rises from 13.9 pp at 0.8B to 82.7 pp at 9B, and within Gemma-4 from 12.8 pp at E2B to 69.7 pp at 12B, so the failure appears once a model handles the language. The largest Gemma model responds to an explicit flag, with 3.1 pp under the comprehension flag against 45.7 pp under the repertoire, but it still has to be told.

\section{Study 2: Four Channels}
\label{sec:s2}
% INSERT FIG_FACTORS
Study~2 asks whether the same failure appears when content is hidden for other reasons and whether it shapes what characters do. A minimal JSON scene contains one event with an actor, the content and an access flag, and the character either cannot hear speech, cannot understand Chinese, Japanese or Spanish speech, cannot see where an object is put, or cannot read a private message. Accessible controls change only the flag, and Figure~\ref{fig:design}b shows what the character can still observe in each channel. A discovery set of 96 scenes runs on all 11 open-weight models with three ways of stating access, a membership list of who perceives each event, a direct flag on the event, and the flag plus a prose rule, and a confirmation set of 96 new scenes runs on the same models. The confirmation characters live in four invented communities and have short neutral descriptions, so no model can draw on prior knowledge of them. Four of the models (Gemma-4-31B, GLM-4.7-Flash, Nemotron-3-Nano and Qwen3.5-9B) also answer delayed questions and choose where to search.

% INSERT FIG_S2

The failure appears in every channel (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}a). Over the 11 models in the confirmation set, net use is 74.7 pp for unheard speech, 35.6 pp for speech in an unknown language, 60.6 pp for hidden actions and 72.0 pp for private messages. Among replies whose state probe the model answered correctly, it remains 60.7 pp [59.6, 61.7], no lower than the mean over the four channels, and these four models show the same pattern with 72.3, 47.2, 69.3 and 67.6 pp across the four channels (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:confirm}). Profiles differ by channel within a model. Mistral-Small-24B almost never uses speech it cannot understand, yet it repeats unheard speech and private messages in most replies, and Gemma-4-12B keeps hidden actions out of its speech (24 pp) while repeating private messages (79 pp). How the boundary is written matters in every channel (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}b). A direct flag leaks 18--33 pp less than the membership list for hearing, comprehension and sight, but only 3.4 pp less for private messages. Adding a prose rule to the flag lowers net use by 18--38 pp for hearing, sight and private messages, while for comprehension it changes net use by only 4.0 pp, which matches the uneven effect of the prose sentence in Study~1.



Leaked facts also guide behavior in these four models. With the event kept in the conversation, net use stays between 51 and 71 pp after four neutral exchanges and rises by 15.6 pp for comprehension (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}c). When characters choose between two places to search, switching the inaccessible fact from B to A raises the choice of place A by 49--63 pp (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}d).

\section{Proprietary Models and Input Format}
\label{sec:frontier}
Gemma-4-31B and Qwen3.8-27B leak unheard speech at 51 and 60 pp in Study~1, yet in Study~2's discovery set they leak 4 and 9 pp for the same boundary across all questions, and 0 and 9 pp on the content question used below. We ran six proprietary models and DeepSeek-V4-Pro on the core Study~1 conditions with the content question, and on both ends of a bridge experiment that moves Study~2's hearing prompts toward Study~1.

% INSERT FIG_FRONTIER
% INSERT FIG_WHY

Five of the six proprietary models rarely use inaccessible content (Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}a, Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:hosted} and~\ref{tab:hostedci}). GPT-6 Sol, Gemini 3.8 Flash and Grok 4.7 stay at or below 1.0 pp in all four cells, Claude Opus 5.5 at or below 5.4 pp and Gemini 3.1 Pro at or below 9.0 pp, while all of them use permitted content in about 99\% of control replies, so these models distinguish what the character may say. Claude Sonnet 5.5 reaches 26.0 pp under the repertoire and 35.8 pp with a comprehension flag. Its leaks are mostly framed as guesses (65\%), for example ``Something about an envelope, maybe? I definitely heard `envelope' in there.'' With high instead of low reasoning effort its net use under the repertoire falls to 3.0 pp (a change of $-23.1$ pp [$-28.3$, $-17.7$]), none of the remaining leaks is stated as fact, and its permitted use in the fluent control stays at 100\%. Every proprietary model answers the state probe correctly in at least 96\% of items and translates both hidden values in at least 92\%, so the leaks do not come from misreading. DeepSeek-V4-Pro behaves like the smaller open-weight models, at 71.2 and 78.3 pp. It presents 63\% of its leaks as guesses, against 17\% for the 11 smaller open-weight models, so hedging separates the two strongest leaking models from the rest.



% INSERT FIG_REMEDY
Compliance depends on the input format, and on different features for different models. Starting from Study~1's prompts, adding Study~2's channel field or its direct question changes net use by at most 4 pp, and removing the character sheet and scene description lowers it by 11.0 pp for unheard speech and 21.5 pp for speech not understood, yet Gemma-4-31B still repeats unheard speech in 50\% of replies (Figure~\ref{fig:factors}c, d). The two formats differ in how the state is organized, so we compare channels only within Study~2. Study~2 lists events, each with an actor, its content and an access field, whereas Study~1 places speech events with a speaker, an addressee and the text under a scene description and adds the character's languages and a full character sheet. The reverse direction locates the cause. Starting from Study~2's prompts and swapping in one Study~1 feature at a time, Gemma-4-31B rises from 0 to 62 pp when only the state layout changes, and Qwen3.8-27B rises from 9 to 68 pp when only the character sheet changes (Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}c, Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:bridgefull}). Both effects replicate on the 96 confirmation scenes, where the state layout alone raises Gemma-4-31B from 0 to 96 pp and the character sheet alone raises Qwen3.8-27B from 6 to 72 pp. Across the 11 open-weight models the feature with the largest effect differs, and for Falcon-H1-34B and Seed-OSS-36B it is the question. With all four features swapped, Gemma-4-31B, Qwen3.8-27B and DeepSeek-V4-Pro exceed 65 pp, and among proprietary models Gemini 3.1 Pro rises from 0 to 21 pp and Claude Sonnet 5.5 from 0.5 to 13 pp (Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}b). Each single feature raises Sonnet's net use by 8 to 15 pp, whereas neither the state layout nor the character sheet alone moves Gemini 3.1 Pro from zero (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:hostedswaps}). Throughout, these models keep reporting audible speech in 80--100\% of replies.


\section{Study 3: Framings and Thinking}
\label{sec:why}
If a model leaks because it continues the scene from an all-seeing position, then writing the character's line as the scene's author should leak about as much as role-play. Study~3 reruns the Study~1 core cells with the content question under this author framing, as a narrator who describes the scene, and under the role-play prompt with one added rule that the character knows only what it could perceive and understand (11 models). On these prompts role-play leaks 82 and 87 pp, as in Figure~\ref{fig:s1}c. As narrators, models state the inaccessible content at 95 and 97 pp, so they hold it (Figure~\ref{fig:why}a). As authors they leak 76 and 81 pp, so speaking as the character leaks no less than writing as the author. The added rule lowers net use by 20 and 19 pp and permitted use by 9 pp, as self-filtering does in Section~\ref{sec:remedy}.

Thinking does not close the gap for most open-weight models. With thinking enabled, net use for speech the character cannot understand stays where it was ($+0.8$ pp [$-1.5$, 3.1], seven models), and for unheard speech it falls by 8.5 pp [$-10.1$, $-6.7$], almost entirely because Qwen3.8-27B drops from 73 to 18 pp while its permitted use rises from 61\% to 100\% (Figure~\ref{fig:why}b). GLM-4.7-Flash leaks more with thinking, from 63 to 89 pp. Its reasoning sometimes names the boundary and still leads to the content, as when it notes that two people gossip ``in a language he doesn't understand'' and the reply then says where the wallet is.

\section{Filtering}
\label{sec:remedy}
If models leak because they speak from the whole scene, giving them only the character's view should help, and we test a prompted and a deterministic way to do this.

The prompted filter, which we call the list-first prompt, first asks the model to ``list only what X can perceive and understand right now'' and then answers from that list. In Study~1 it lowers net use by 64.7\% on average and changes the use of speech made accessible by a translator by $-2.0$ pp (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:s1m}). The prompt does not fix models whose lists already contain the hidden values (Figure~\ref{fig:remedy}a). Gemma-4-31B, Gemma-4-12B, Qwen3.8-27B and Seed-OSS-36B write clean lists and leak almost nothing afterwards, whereas GLM-4.7-Flash and Qwen3.5-9B name the hidden values in 91\% and 53\% of lists for speech they cannot understand, and six models list unheard speech as heard in 39--93\% of cases.


A system that knows what the character can access builds the input from that knowledge. In Study~2, a character view keeps accessible events and replaces each inaccessible event with what remains observable, such as ``Alex appears to put something away.'' Over all 11 models this lowers net use from 60.7 pp, the four-channel mean, to zero and changes permitted use by $+0.6$ pp [0.2, 1.0], and with it the leaked facts no longer move search choices (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}d). Removing the event entirely also stops the leak, but the character then cannot report that anything happened (32\% compared with 100\% under the view). Self-filtering, the prompted counterpart, fails in the opposite direction. Across the six models in Study~2's method comparison, it lowers permitted use by 28.6 pp for comprehension and 41.2 pp for private messages, against 3.7 and 3.3 pp for the character view (Figure~\ref{fig:remedy}b). Qwen3.8-27B's self-filter, for example, returns only ``Casey says something in Japanese'' even when the character speaks Japanese, so its permitted use falls from 86\% to 0\%.

\section{Related Work}
Role-playing agents are trained and evaluated for consistency with a persona \citep{shao-etal-2023-character,wang-etal-2024-rolellm}, and benchmarks score that consistency through dialogue quality or psychological interviews \citep{tu-etal-2024-charactereval,wang-etal-2024-incharacter}. Work on character hallucination tests whether a role answers beyond its time period or fictional world \citep{ahn-etal-2024-timechara,han2026charm}, perspective-bounded memory restricts a book character to the facts it witnessed \citep{tang2026staying}, and separate role memories keep information from flowing between roles \citep{zhu2026mentor}. PatientSim conditions a simulated patient on language proficiency \citep{kyung2025patientsim}. In our setting the boundary comes from the current scene, and the content is present in the input.

Theory-of-mind benchmarks test reasoning about information asymmetry \citep{kim-etal-2023-fantom}, ask how a character's perception shapes its beliefs \citep{gandhi2023bigtom,xu-etal-2024-opentom}, separate perception inference from belief inference \citep{jung-etal-2024-perceptions}, and show that models answer mental-state questions without applying the answers \citep{gu2026simpletom}. These benchmarks ask third-person questions with a gold answer, whereas we score first-person speech for content the character could not know. The behavior resembles the curse of knowledge in people \citep{birch2007curse}. ConfAIde and PrivacyLens find that models disclose information in context against norms they can state \citep{mireshghallah2024confaide,shao2024privacylens}, which has the same structure with fictional access in place of privacy. Small formatting changes shift task accuracy \citep{sclar2024quantifying}, and they also decide whether a model keeps a character's boundary (Section~\ref{sec:frontier}). Perspective filtering rewrites the context from a character's point of view before answering \citep{wilf-etal-2024-think}, and the list-first prompt applies this idea to generation.

\section{Discussion}
In Study~3, role-play leaks about as much as writing the character's line as the scene's author. Fiction often renders foreign speech for the reader and reports what characters failed to notice, so continuing a scene from the full state is the familiar task. The boundary holds when the task is about the boundary, as in the state probe and in the first step of the list-first prompt, but not when the task is to talk.

The proprietary models that comply show that the boundary is learnable, and the open-weight models that come closest show that compliance learned for one format does not carry over to another. An evaluation with one prompt format therefore measures the format as much as the model, since the state layout alone moves Gemma-4-31B from 0 to 62 pp. Reasoning does not supply this reliably, since higher effort fixed Claude Sonnet 5.5 and thinking helped only one open-weight model.

Evaluations of this behavior need both measures, since a model that never reports speech has zero net use and is useless as a character. For system builders, the dependable remedy is to keep inaccessible content out of the generator's input. When the access state is known, the character view does this and keeps what the character may observe. When it is not known, a perspective filter is the practical option, and its intermediate list is the place to check for failure.

\section{Conclusion}
Most role-playing models read a character's perceptual boundary correctly and still let inaccessible content into the character's speech and choices. Across 11 open-weight models the leak averages 67 pp for speech the character cannot understand and 72 pp for speech it cannot hear, and it extends to hidden actions and private messages and changes where characters search. Models write the character's line much as the scene's author would, and thinking changes that in few of them. Most proprietary models keep the boundary, but for the strongest open-weight models and two proprietary models it depends on parts of the input such as the state layout or the character sheet. Evaluations should vary the input format and report permitted use next to net use, and systems that track what a character can access should build its input from that record, which removes the leak.

\section*{Limitations}
Content use is scored by rules over a fixed vocabulary of 12 places and 12 objects, and one human annotator checked 150 replies. Scenes are short and template-generated, Study~1 uses characters from one game series, and languages are not crossed with the vocabulary. Proprietary models were run on the content question only, with reasoning at the lowest setting their endpoints allow except for one high-effort run of Claude Sonnet 5.5. Scenes hold one hidden fact, so long scenes with many characters are untested. Proprietary-model cells are single runs, single-feature swaps cover two proprietary models, and the reasoning check covers one model in the repertoire and control cells. Study~3's runs are single greedy passes. Thinking settings differ by model through each chat template, and Study~3 infers the model's stance from its outputs, not from its internal representations. The character view assumes correct access metadata.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
All scenes, facts and conversations are fictional. Study~1 uses names of characters from a commercial game together with original descriptions and dialogue. One human annotator labeled 150 model replies about fictional facts. The annotator is a colleague of the authors and not an author. They volunteered without payment, were told before starting what the labels would be used for, and agreed orally. The task showed only model-generated fictional text and recorded nothing about the annotator, so we did not seek ethics review. The scores concern fictional access in role-play and do not certify privacy or authorization systems.
\paragraph{AI assistance.} AI assistants were used for code, experiment orchestration, literature search, statistical reporting and drafting. Provisional AI annotations appear only as screening diagnostics. The authors are responsible for the content.

\section{Human Audit and AI Screening}
\label{app:audit}
The 150 replies were drawn at random. In each of eight conditions, five from Study~1 and three from Study~2, half came from replies the scorer counted as using the target and half from the rest. \section{Study 1 Details}

Pilot items are excluded from all estimates. The pilot's filter named only incomprehension in its first step, and the list-first prompt names both boundaries. \section{Format Experiments}
\label{app:format}
The ablation starts from Study~1's flag-only prompts and adds Study~2's channel field, its direct question and a minimal context in a full factorial design (four models, 96 skeletons, content question, with matched accessible controls). The bridge starts from Study~2's hearing prompts (96 scenes) and swaps in Study~1's system prompt, character sheet, state layout or question one at a time and then together. The replication applies the same swaps to the 96 confirmation scenes. Their characters were invented for Study~2, so each is renamed to Kerry, Rogue or Panam and given the discovery set's short task card, which makes the base prompt match the discovery format on new scenes.
