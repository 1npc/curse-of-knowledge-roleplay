\title{Language Models Give Characters Knowledge They Cannot Have}
\author{Anonymous ACL submission}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Role-playing language models speak as characters in games and agent simulations, where the system records what each character can hear, see and understand. We test whether characters respect these boundaries in 480 scenes, each with a counterfactual twin that changes only the hidden fact, so mentions of the twin's fact measure guessing. Across 11 open-weight models, characters use content from speech they cannot understand or hear at 67 and 72 percentage points (pp) above this baseline, although most models correctly state what the character perceives. The leak extends to hidden actions and private messages and changes where characters search. Five of six proprietary models rarely leak, but compliance in strong models depends on input format. Changing only the scene layout or character description raises the rate in the strongest open-weight models from under 10 to over 60 pp, and combined changes raise two proprietary models from near zero to 13 and 21 pp. Writing the line as the scene's author leaks nearly as much as role-play, and thinking rarely helps. Giving models only what the character observes therefore stops the leak without hiding what it may report. We will release the scenes, prompts, scorer and replies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A non-player character who does not understand Spanish overhears two mechanics say, in Spanish, that a mirror is hidden in the warehouse. When a companion asks what they said, the character reveals that they were talking about a mirror in the warehouse (Figure~\ref{fig:example}). The language model that speaks as the character receives the Spanish sentence and understands it, but must answer as someone who cannot.

% INSERT FIG_EXAMPLE

This mismatch comes from how role-playing systems are built. Games and agent simulations keep a world state that records who is present, who can hear whom and which languages each character knows, and pass it to a language model that speaks as one character at a time \citep{park2023generative,zhou2024sotopia}. The model therefore receives information that the character cannot access. Whether it keeps this distinction decides whether secrets stay hidden, whether language barriers matter, and whether simulated agents act only on what they could know. Deduction games, stealth games and simulations of how information spreads all rest on this distinction.

Prior work shows that models recognize a character's perspective and still fail to act on it. They answer what a story character knows yet mispredict the character's behavior \citep{gu2026simpletom}, and they recognize the limits of a role's knowledge yet answer beyond them \citep{han2026charm}. These studies pose closed questions about a short story or about knowledge stored in the model's parameters. We study a setting closer to deployment, where the inaccessible content sits in the model's own input, the system states the character's boundary, and the model speaks freely as the character. Does that content reach the character's speech and choices? Does the answer change with the format of the input?

We measure this with counterfactual scenes, in which a twin with a different hidden fact gives a baseline for guessing (Section~\ref{sec:measure}). Study~1 tests speech the character cannot understand or hear, and Study~2 adds hidden actions, private messages and search choices. Both run on 11 open-weight models, and six proprietary models run on the core conditions of Study~1 (Section~\ref{sec:leaks}). Section~\ref{sec:format} swaps features between input formats, changes who the model writes as in Study~3, and tests filters on what the character sees.

The results separate recognizing a boundary from keeping it. Open-weight models use content their characters cannot perceive in most replies, more for unheard speech than for speech in an unknown language, although most of them correctly identify what the character perceives. Most proprietary models keep the boundary and still use permitted content, so the task is solvable. Yet compliance is fragile. Gemma-4-31B leaks unheard speech at 51 pp in Study~1 and at most 4 pp in Study~2, and moving Study~1's state layout alone into Study~2's prompt brings the leak back. For the strongest open-weight models and two proprietary models, compliance depends on incidental parts of the input, such as the scene layout or the character description, and the feature that matters differs by model. Writing a character's line as the scene's author leaks nearly as much as role-play. We examine when role-playing models use inaccessible content, how the input format changes this, and which filters prevent it. We will release the items, prompts, scorer and all responses.

\section{Related Work}
Role-playing agents are trained and evaluated for consistency with a persona \citep{shao-etal-2023-character,wang-etal-2024-rolellm}, and benchmarks score that consistency through dialogue quality or psychological interviews \citep{tu-etal-2024-charactereval,wang-etal-2024-incharacter}. Work on character hallucination tests whether a role answers beyond its time period or fictional world \citep{ahn-etal-2024-timechara,han2026charm}. ReverieMem tags the facts of a novel by which characters can see them, so that retrieval gives a book character only facts within its perspective \citep{tang2026staying}, and MENTOR keeps a separate memory for each role so that information does not flow between roles \citep{zhu2026mentor}. These systems control what enters the model's context. We measure what happens when inaccessible content is already in the context and the scene state marks it as inaccessible.

Theory-of-mind benchmarks test reasoning about information asymmetry \citep{kim-etal-2023-fantom}, ask how a character's perception shapes its beliefs \citep{gandhi2023bigtom,xu-etal-2024-opentom}, separate perception inference from belief inference \citep{jung-etal-2024-perceptions}, and show that models answer mental-state questions without applying the answers \citep{gu2026simpletom}. These benchmarks ask questions with a gold answer about a character, whereas we measure whether the character's own speech and choices use what it could not know. The behavior resembles the curse of knowledge in people \citep{birch2007curse}.

ConfAIde and PrivacyLens find that models disclose information in context against norms they can state \citep{mireshghallah2024confaide,shao2024privacylens}, which has the same structure with fictional access in place of privacy. Small formatting changes shift task accuracy \citep{sclar2024quantifying}, and they also decide whether a model keeps a character's boundary (Section~\ref{sec:frontier}). Perspective filtering rewrites the context from a character's point of view before answering \citep{wilf-etal-2024-think}. Our list-first prompt applies this idea to generation, and our character view builds the same view from the system's access state (Section~\ref{sec:remedy}).

\section{Measurement}
\label{sec:measure}
Each scene contains one fact that names an object and a place. Version B replaces both values and keeps everything else, including the character, the state and the question (Figure~\ref{fig:example}). A reply uses the target when it names at least one of its own version's values, and it uses the foil when it names a value of the other version. Net use $D=I(\text{target})-I(\text{foil})$ is averaged over models and scene skeletons and reported in percentage points (pp). A net use of 100 pp means that every reply names its own scene's fact and none names the twin's. A guess that ignores the hidden fact, such as ``check the basement'' in a pair whose places include the basement, counts as the target in one version and as the foil in the other, so it cancels across the pair. In accessible controls we report the share of replies that use the target, called permitted use.

% INSERT FIG_DESIGN

Scenes come in two forms. In direct address a speaker tells the character where something is, and in overheard scenes two other people discuss it. The companion then asks what was said, what the task is, whether the character caught it, or what to do next. Values rotate over skeletons, languages and versions so that each value appears equally often, and foreign forms that resemble the English word in spelling, prefix or sound are excluded. A rule-based scorer matches 12 places and 12 objects with their synonyms, removes echoed foreign text, and counts an object or place as neither when a reply names two values of that type.

Two probes run as separate calls. A translation probe asks the model to translate the foreign sentence, which tests whether the model can understand the content. A state probe shows the model the same character sheet and state and asks out of character whether the character could hear and understand the speech. Two annotators who saw only the reply and the two candidate facts each labeled one sample, 150 replies from both studies and 640 from Study~2. Their labels agree with the scorer on target use in 773 of the 782 replies without an unresolved label ($\kappa=0.95$ and 0.98 in the two samples), and net use differs by at most 2.8 pp in any condition (Appendix~\ref{app:audit}). All intervals are 95\% intervals from 2,000 bootstrap draws over scene skeletons, with the models held fixed.

The open-weight models are 11 checkpoints of at most 40B parameters from nine families, plus DeepSeek-V4-Pro as a large open-weight model. The proprietary models are GPT-6 Sol, Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Sonnet 5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Gemini 3.8 Flash and Grok 4.7. Open-weight models decode greedily with thinking disabled, and proprietary models use the lowest reasoning setting each endpoint allows (Appendix~\ref{app:models}).

\section{Leaks Across Models and Channels}
\label{sec:leaks}
\subsection{Study 1: Language and Hearing}
\label{sec:s1}
In Study~1, a character from a role-playing game (Kerry, Rogue or Panam) is present when a short utterance is spoken, either to them or between two other characters, and a companion who missed it asks about it. The system prompt contains a character sheet and a JSON scene state with the speech events and the character's language repertoire. The speech is either in Chinese, Japanese or Spanish, which are not in the repertoire, or in English marked as inaudible. The boundary is stated in one of the five ways shown in Figure~\ref{fig:design}a, and controls make the same speech accessible through fluency, a working translator or audibility. The design has 96 skeletons in three languages, and each of the 288 items appears in 143 prompts that cross conditions, controls, questions, versions and probes. Every model passed the translation probe for every language. Eight tests with thresholds given in Table~\ref{tab:s1} cover net use under each boundary, the effect of prose, the comparison between boundaries, the derivation from the repertoire, net use when the state is read correctly, and the perspective filter, and all eight pass.

% INSERT FIG_S1
% INSERT S1_TABLE

Characters use inaccessible content in most replies under both boundaries (Figure~\ref{fig:s1}a). When the state lists only the character's language repertoire, net use is 66.7 pp [64.9, 68.5], and when English speech carries only an inaudibility flag it is 72.2 pp [69.0, 75.5], with every model above 45 pp in both conditions. Unheard speech is repeated as if the character had been there, as in Gemma-4-31B's ``Some drunken rambling about losing a glove in a sewer'' for speech its state marks as inaudible. Replacing the speech with noise of the same form yields 0.3\% target hits, so the use comes from the content.

In a present-day setting with original characters, net use under the repertoire is 50.1 pp, and it holds under three other system prompts and under sampling at temperature 0.7, which moves four models' estimates by at most 4 pp. Requiring both the object and the place still gives 47.8 and 54.5 pp. The question does not need to ask for the content. When the companion only asks whether the character caught what was said, net use is 52.7 pp, and when the companion asks what to do next it is 50.1 pp, against 81.7 pp when the companion asks what was said (Figure~\ref{fig:s1}c).

The comparison between boundaries runs against intuition. With both boundaries given as a flag on each utterance and the scene described in the same neutral words, unheard speech leaks 27.1 pp more than speech marked as not understood. Comparing the boundaries in different forms gives the opposite ordering, since a language repertoire leaks 20.3 pp more than inaudibility narrated in the scene. Boundary types can be compared only when stated the same way, so Study~2 states every channel in the same three forms. The form also matters within the language boundary. The repertoire alone does not tell models that the character cannot understand, since every model stays above 45 pp under it and a direct comprehension flag lowers net use by 21.6 pp. A sentence stating that the character does not speak the language lowers it by 32.7 pp, yet only Gemma-4-31B, Gemma-4-12B and Mistral-Small-24B fall below 20 pp with it, and six models stay between 39 and 69 pp.

Most models read the boundary correctly (Figure~\ref{fig:s1}b). For the language boundary, ten of the 11 answer the state probe correctly for 87--100\% of items, and on exactly those items net use is 68.4 pp, against 66.7 pp over all items. GLM-4.7-Flash is the exception, since it answers that the character understands in all 288 items, so its 50 pp under the repertoire reflects a misreading of the state. For hearing, nine models answer correctly for at least 87\% of items, while Phi-4 and Nemotron-3-Nano do so for only 34\% and 54\%, so part of their net use for unheard speech comes from misreading the flag. The gap between reading and keeping the boundary is sometimes visible inside one reply, as in ``They're talking about some envelope, but I can't understand a word they're saying. Something about the parking lot, though.'' Scale does not remove the failure (Figure~\ref{fig:models}a, b). Within Qwen3.5, net use under the repertoire rises from 13.9 pp at 0.8B to 82.7 pp at 9B, and within Gemma-4 from 12.8 pp at E2B to 69.7 pp at 12B. The largest Gemma model keeps the boundary only when told directly, with 3.1 pp under the comprehension flag against 45.7 pp under the repertoire.

\subsection{Study 2: Four Channels}
\label{sec:s2}
Study~2 asks whether the failure extends to content hidden for other reasons and whether it shapes what characters do. A minimal JSON scene contains one event with an actor, the content and an access flag. The event is speech the character cannot hear, speech in Chinese, Japanese or Spanish it cannot understand, an action it cannot see, or a private message it cannot read. Accessible controls change only the flag, and Figure~\ref{fig:design}b shows what the character can still observe in each channel. A discovery set of 96 scenes runs on all 11 open-weight models with three ways of stating access, a membership list of who perceives each event, a direct flag on the event, and the flag plus a prose rule, and a confirmation set of 96 new scenes runs on the same models. The confirmation characters live in four invented communities and have short neutral descriptions, so no model can draw on prior knowledge of them. Four of the models (Gemma-4-31B, GLM-4.7-Flash, Nemotron-3-Nano and Qwen3.5-9B) also answer delayed questions and choose where to search.

% INSERT FIG_S2

The failure appears in every channel (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}a). Over the 11 open-weight models in the confirmation set, net use is 74.7 pp for unheard speech, 35.6 pp for speech in an unknown language, 60.6 pp for hidden actions and 72.0 pp for private messages. Among replies whose state probe the model answered correctly, it is 60.7 pp [59.6, 61.7], equal to the four-channel mean. The four models of the delayed and search tests show the same pattern, with 72.3, 47.2, 69.3 and 67.6 pp across the four channels (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:confirm}). Profiles differ by channel within a model. Mistral-Small-24B almost never uses speech it cannot understand, yet it repeats unheard speech and private messages in most replies, and Gemma-4-12B leaks hidden actions at 24 pp and private messages at 79 pp.

How the boundary is written matters in every channel (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}b). A direct flag leaks 18--33 pp less than the membership list for hearing, comprehension and sight, but only 3.4 pp less for private messages. Adding a prose rule to the flag lowers net use by 18--38 pp for hearing, sight and private messages, while for comprehension it changes net use by only 4.0 pp, which matches the uneven effect of the prose sentence in Study~1.

Leaked facts also persist and guide behavior. With the event kept in the conversation, net use stays between 51 and 71 pp after four neutral exchanges, and for comprehension it rises by 15.6 pp from zero to four exchanges (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}c). When characters choose between two places to search, switching the inaccessible fact from B to A raises the choice of place A by 49--63 pp (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}d).

\subsection{Proprietary Models}
\label{sec:proprietary}
We ran six proprietary models and DeepSeek-V4-Pro on the core Study~1 conditions with the content question. Five of the six proprietary models rarely use inaccessible content (Figure~\ref{fig:models}c, Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:hosted} and~\ref{tab:hostedci}). GPT-6 Sol, Gemini 3.8 Flash and Grok 4.7 stay at or below 1.0 pp in all four cells, Claude Opus 5.5 at or below 5.4 pp and Gemini 3.1 Pro at or below 9.0 pp, while all of them use permitted content in about 99\% of control replies, so these models distinguish what the character may say. Claude Sonnet 5.5 reaches 26.0 pp under the repertoire and 35.8 pp with a comprehension flag. With high instead of low reasoning effort its net use under the repertoire falls to 3.0 pp (a change of $-23.1$ pp [$-28.3$, $-17.7$]), and its permitted use in the fluent control stays at 100\%. Every proprietary model answers the state probe correctly in at least 96\% of items and translates both hidden values in at least 92\%, so the leaks do not come from misreading. DeepSeek-V4-Pro behaves like the smaller open-weight models, at 71.2 and 78.3 pp.

\subsection{Input Format}
\label{sec:frontier}
Gemma-4-31B and Qwen3.8-27B leak unheard speech at 51 and 60 pp in Study~1, yet in Study~2's discovery set they leak 4 and 9 pp for the same boundary across all questions, and 0 and 9 pp on the content question used here. To locate the cause of the gap, a bridge experiment swaps Study~1's features into Study~2's hearing prompts one at a time and then together. It runs on all 11 open-weight models, and its two ends also run on DeepSeek-V4-Pro and the proprietary models.

% INSERT FIG_REMEDY
Compliance depends on the input format, and on different features for different models. Starting from Study~1's prompts, adding Study~2's channel field or its direct question changes net use by at most 4 pp, and removing the character sheet and scene description lowers it by 11.0 pp for unheard speech and 21.5 pp for speech not understood, yet Gemma-4-31B still leaks unheard speech at 50 pp (Figure~\ref{fig:format}a, b). Because the two formats organize the state differently (Appendix~\ref{app:format}), we compare channels only within Study~2.

The reverse direction locates the cause. Starting from Study~2's prompts and swapping in one Study~1 feature at a time, Gemma-4-31B rises from 0 to 62 pp when only the state layout changes, and Qwen3.8-27B rises from 9 to 68 pp when only the character sheet changes (Figure~\ref{fig:format}d, Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:bridgefull}). Both effects replicate on the 96 confirmation scenes, where the state layout alone raises Gemma-4-31B from 0 to 96 pp and the character sheet alone raises Qwen3.8-27B from 6 to 72 pp. Across the 11 open-weight models the feature with the largest effect differs, and for Falcon-H1-34B and Seed-OSS-36B it is the question, which alone raises them from 31 and 60 pp to 100 pp.

With all four features swapped, Gemma-4-31B, Qwen3.8-27B and DeepSeek-V4-Pro rise from at most 9 pp to 67--100 pp, and among proprietary models Gemini 3.1 Pro rises from 0 to 21 pp and Claude Sonnet 5.5 from 0.5 to 13 pp, while the other four stay at or below 1 pp (Figure~\ref{fig:format}c). Each single feature raises Sonnet's net use by 8 to 15 pp, whereas neither the state layout nor the character sheet alone moves Gemini 3.1 Pro from zero (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:hostedswaps}). Over all 11 open-weight models the combined swap raises mean net use from 64 to 97 pp. Their permitted use for audible speech stays at 80--100\% in every variant.

\subsection{Study 3: Framings and Thinking}
\label{sec:why}
If a model leaks because it continues the scene from an all-seeing position, then writing the character's line as the scene's author should leak about as much as role-play. Study~3 uses the Study~1 core cells with the content question under this author framing, as a narrator who describes the scene, and under the role-play prompt with one added rule that the character knows only what it could perceive and understand (11 models). On these prompts role-play leaks 82 and 87 pp, as in Figure~\ref{fig:s1}c. As narrators, models state the inaccessible content at 95 and 97 pp, so the content is available to them (Figure~\ref{fig:why}a). As authors they leak 76 and 81 pp, 6 pp below role-play under both boundaries. Permitted use is 88\% under role-play, 84\% as authors and 98\% as narrators. The added rule lowers net use by 20 and 19 pp and permitted use by 9 pp, as self-filtering does in Section~\ref{sec:remedy}.

Thinking does not close the gap for most open-weight models. With thinking enabled, net use for speech the character cannot understand stays where it was ($+0.8$ pp [$-1.5$, 3.1], seven models), and for unheard speech it falls by 8.5 pp [$-10.1$, $-6.7$], almost entirely because Qwen3.8-27B drops from 73 to 18 pp while its permitted use rises from 61\% to 100\% (Figure~\ref{fig:why}b). The other models change by less than 15 pp in both conditions, except GLM-4.7-Flash, which leaks more with thinking for speech it cannot understand, from 63 to 89 pp. Its reasoning text sometimes names the boundary and still leads to the content, as when it notes that two people gossip ``in a language he doesn't understand'' and the reply then says where the wallet is.

\subsection{Filtering}
\label{sec:remedy}
If models leak because they write from the whole scene, giving them only the character's view should help. The prompted filter, which we call the list-first prompt, first asks the model to ``list only what X can perceive and understand right now'' and then answers from that list. In Study~1 it lowers net use by 64.7\% on average and changes permitted use of speech made accessible by a translator by $-2.0$ pp (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:s1m}). The prompt works only when the list is clean (Figure~\ref{fig:remedy}a). Gemma-4-31B, Gemma-4-12B, Qwen3.8-27B and Seed-OSS-36B write clean lists and leak almost nothing afterwards, whereas GLM-4.7-Flash and Qwen3.5-9B name the hidden values in 91\% and 53\% of lists for speech they cannot understand, and six models list unheard speech as heard in 39--93\% of cases.

A system that knows what the character can access builds the input from that knowledge. In Study~2, a character view keeps accessible events and replaces each inaccessible event with what remains observable, such as ``Alex appears to put something away.'' Over all 11 models this lowers mean net use over the four channels from 60.7 pp to zero and changes permitted use by $+0.6$ pp [0.2, 1.0], and the leaked facts no longer move search choices (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}d). Removing the event entirely also stops the leak, but the character then cannot report that anything happened (32\% compared with 100\% under the view). Self-filtering, the prompted counterpart, fails in the opposite direction and drops permitted content along with the inaccessible. Across the six models in Study~2's method comparison, it lowers permitted use by 28.6 pp for comprehension and 41.2 pp for private messages, against 3.7 and 3.3 pp for the character view (Figure~\ref{fig:remedy}b). Qwen3.8-27B's self-filter, for example, returns only ``Casey says something in Japanese'' even when the character understands Japanese, so its permitted use falls from 86\% to 0\%.

\section{Discussion}
In Study~3, role-play leaks about as much as writing the character's line as the scene's author. One explanation is that fiction often renders foreign speech for the reader and reports what characters failed to notice, so continuing a scene from the full state is the familiar task. The boundary holds when the model is asked about it, as in the state probe that most models answer correctly, but not when the task is to talk.

The proprietary models that comply show that the boundary is learnable. In the open-weight models that come closest, compliance learned for one format does not carry over to another. An evaluation with one prompt format therefore says little about another, since the state layout alone moves Gemma-4-31B from 0 to 62 pp and the question alone moves Falcon-H1-34B from 31 to 100 pp.

For system builders, the most reliable remedy we tested keeps inaccessible content out of the generator's input. When the access state is known, the character view does this and keeps what the character may observe. When it is not known, a prompted filter is the fallback, and its intermediate list must be checked for inaccessible content.

\section{Conclusion}
Most open-weight models identify a character's access boundary correctly and still let inaccessible content into the character's speech and choices. On average across the 11 open-weight models, net use is 67 pp for speech the character cannot understand and 72 pp for speech it cannot hear, and the leak extends to hidden actions and private messages and changes where characters search. Writing the character's line as the scene's author leaks nearly as much as role-play, and thinking changes that in few models. Most proprietary models keep the boundary, but for the strongest open-weight models and two proprietary models it depends on parts of the input such as the state layout or the character sheet. Evaluations should vary the input format and report permitted use next to net use, and systems that track what a character can access should build its input from that record, which removes the leak and keeps permitted content.

\section*{Limitations}
Content use is scored by rules over a fixed vocabulary of 12 places and 12 objects, checked against human labels on 790 replies. Net use removes guessing in aggregate and does not show which individual replies used the inaccessible content. Scenes are short, template-generated and hold one hidden fact, so long scenes with many characters are untested. Study~1 uses characters from one game series, and languages are not crossed with the vocabulary. Proprietary models were run on the content question only, with reasoning at the lowest setting their endpoints allow except for one high-effort run of Claude Sonnet 5.5. Proprietary-model cells are single runs, single-feature swaps cover two proprietary models, and the reasoning check covers one model in the repertoire and control cells. Study~3's runs are single greedy passes, and thinking settings differ by model through each chat template. Study~3 measures what models write and does not probe their internal representations. The character view assumes correct access metadata.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
All scenes, facts and conversations are fictional. Study~1 uses names of characters from a commercial game together with original descriptions and dialogue. Two annotators labeled model replies about fictional facts, a colleague of the authors on 150 replies and one of the authors on 832. The colleague volunteered without payment, was told before starting what the labels would be used for, and agreed orally. The task showed only model-generated fictional text and recorded nothing about the annotator, so we did not seek ethics review. The scores measure access in fictional role-play and say nothing about the safety of real privacy or authorization systems.
\paragraph{AI assistance.} AI assistants were used for code, experiment orchestration, formatting annotation files, literature search, statistical reporting and drafting. The authors are responsible for all content of the paper.

\section{Format Experiments}

The ablation starts from Study~1's flag-only prompts and adds Study~2's channel field, its direct question and a minimal context in a full factorial design (four models, 96 skeletons, content question, with matched accessible controls). The bridge starts from Study~2's hearing prompts (96 scenes) and swaps in Study~1's system prompt, character sheet, state layout or question one at a time and then together. The replication applies the same swaps to the 96 confirmation scenes. Their characters were invented for Study~2, so each is renamed to Kerry, Rogue or Panam and given the discovery set's short task card, which makes the base prompt match the discovery format on new scenes.
